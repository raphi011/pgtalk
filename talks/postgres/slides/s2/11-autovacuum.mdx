# Autovacuum, and its threshold

<Runnable
  appearAt={1}
  hideAt={4}
  sql={`SELECT name, setting, unit FROM pg_settings
WHERE name IN ('autovacuum_naptime',
               'autovacuum_vacuum_threshold',
               'autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor',
               'autovacuum_max_workers',
               'autovacuum_vacuum_cost_delay')
ORDER BY name;`}
/>

<Note appearAt={2} hideAt={3}>

**Dead tuples ≥ 50 + 0.2 × live rows.** A table is vacuumed once a fifth of it
is garbage — a percentage, not a size.

</Note>

<Note appearAt={3} hideAt={4}>

**So the threshold grows with the table.** 10 000 rows: vacuum at 2 050 dead.
100 million rows: vacuum at 20 million dead, several gigabytes of garbage
tolerated before a worker even starts.

On a big, hot table the scale factor is the first thing to lower — per table,
not globally.

</Note>

<Runnable
  appearAt={4}
  hideAt={6}
  sql={`SELECT reltuples::bigint                            AS live_rows,
       (50 + 0.2 * reltuples)::bigint                 AS vacuum_at,
       reloptions
FROM pg_class WHERE relname = 'orders';`}
/>

<Note appearAt={5} hideAt={6}>

**And this table is opted out entirely.** `autovacuum_enabled = off`, set by
this deck's fixture so the last three slides could hold still. In production
that setting is a loaded gun: nothing warns you that a table is no longer
being maintained.

</Note>

<Runnable appearAt={6} hideAt={7} sql="ANALYZE orders;" />

<Runnable
  appearAt={7}
  sql={`SELECT relname, n_live_tup, n_dead_tup, last_autovacuum, autovacuum_count
FROM pg_stat_user_tables
WHERE relname IN ('orders', 'customers', 'order_items');`}
/>

<Note appearAt={8}>

**This is the table to watch in production.** `n_dead_tup` against
`n_live_tup`, and `last_autovacuum` — a timestamp that is hours old on a busy
table means the worker cannot keep up, and the cost delay is the reason.

`orders` reports as much garbage as data here — 500 000 of each. `customers`
and `order_items` report nothing at all, because a fixture restore copies the
data and not the statistics, and only `orders` has been analysed since.

</Note>
